The arc

I started carrying a personal quota, moved to running commercial organizations, and for the last decade I have owned revenue numbers at company scale. Nineteen years, four continents, four industries: Medtech, Adtech, Fintech and B2B SaaS.

The constant is not a playbook. It is starting with how customers choose, adopt and get value, and building the commercial system accordingly.

How the results happen

Acquire the right customers.
Help them win with your product.
Make getting value easier.

The rule underneath it is one I have used since Amazon: bring a person in when automation and mass communication are not enough. Done well, the cost of serving a customer stops tracking the revenue they produce.

At Taboola it took the unit from about $130M to $500M without scaling the team at the same rate. At Rapyd it became automated merchant onboarding. At Lokalise it became being the answer a buyer's AI gives.

Helping customers win, and making value easier to get, are exactly what AI now does more cost-effectively. That is why the shift below is a commercial challenge, not just a technical one.

Current point of view

The part of the customer journey your dashboards barely register is getting larger.

Customers have been getting further on their own for a decade: finding you, comparing you, connecting you, running the product, fixing it when it breaks. What is changing is who is doing it. Increasingly it is a machine, and how far it gets depends on two things: how far your customers have gone in handing work to AI, and how legible your product is to it. That distance is your serviceable line, and it is the share of the market, and of each customer's journey, you can win without adding people.

The serviceable line is the boundary between what your product, content, and interfaces can carry on their own, and what still costs you a person. There are five of these lines, and AI is moving each of them at a different speed.

What makes this urgent is the pace. More of the buying journey and more of the customer experience is AI-mediated every quarter, and go-to-market practices built for an entirely human journey are being disrupted faster than most teams are re-tooling.

The operating test

How much work one person could carry once AI was connected to real context and tools, and where that leverage stopped.

I built and run a working personal AI operating system at the smallest useful scale: one operator, no team, real costs and real consequences. It works across family, household, travel, health, finance, research, writing and development.

The answer only became useful when I added the controls the demo layer skips: scoped permissions, human approval, watchdogs, canary tests, backups, incident logging and a written record of failures.

At Amazon, Taboola and Rapyd, I learned the same lesson while building self-service and PLG businesses: scale depended on how much of the journey a customer could complete without help. The same holds for AI. Progress stops when the customer or operator meets a sales gate, incomplete documentation or a missing API. That is the practical link between the system I built and the serviceable-line point of view above.
